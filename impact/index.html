    Impact: before and after NØMAÐ¶

    A year or more after people start getting NØMAÐ's feedback, the question is what changed: do jobs ask for closer to what they use, do fewer fail or run out of memory, do they wait less? [impact] in nomad.toml records when that feedback began and what else changed along the way; nomad impact compares the jobs before and after.

    [impact]
launch = 2026-10-19            # when people started getting NØMAÐ's feedback
baseline_from = 2025-08-24     # where "before" starts (default: a year before launch)
events = [
  { date = 2026-10-06, what = "Console open to staff" },
  { date = 2027-01-11, what = "four nodes added to the large partition" },
]
exclude_users = ["svc-backup"] # accounts that aren't people (1.13.2)

    launch splits the jobs into before and after. events is a log of everything else that changes how people use the machines: nodes added, a Slurm limit, a workshop, a new part of NØMAÐ itself. Write them down when they happen; a report a year later names them instead of crediting NØMAÐ with what they did. exclude_users names accounts that aren't people (a service account that runs test jobs, signs in to every workstation, or holds a login session open): they are left out of every figure. nomad config check says what in the section it can't use.

    What the before period needs¶

    nomad impact status

    For each site, the jobs that ended before and after launch: how many, how many measured, how many measured the 1.13 way, how many with an eligible time. Then what to run:

    • Jobs the database doesn't reach back to. Slurm's accounting usually keeps far more than nomad has collected. On the site, nomad import sacct --from BASELINE (a dry run, then --apply) brings them, with their eligible times.
    • Measurements read the old way. Before 1.13, CPU use came from Slurm's AveCPU, the average over a step's tasks: a job running 16 tasks read about a sixteenth of its use. Set against 1.13's figures, that would look like an improvement NØMAÐ didn't make. On the site, nomad impact backfill (a dry run, then --apply) reads the before period again from Slurm, a week at a time, and stores CPU and memory as the collector does now. Slurm keeps step usage only as long as slurmdbd's purge settings allow, often far less time than it keeps the jobs; a job whose usage it no longer has keeps what nomad stored (1.13.2), and the status says how much is still unmeasured.

    Both run on each site, not on the hub; the next sync brings the results.

    Bringing back measurements from an older copy¶

    In 1.13.0 and 1.13.1, a job read again without usage (Slurm had purged its steps) was stored as "not measured" over the measurement nomad had taken when the job ended: nomad impact backfill could erase months of them. 1.13.2 never writes an empty reading over a stored one. What was erased can come back from an older copy of the site's database, or of a hub's combined database, from before the backfill:

    nomad impact extract --from OLD/combined.db --site SITE --out SITE-measurements.db
nomad impact restore --from SITE-measurements.db            # on the site: a dry run
nomad impact restore --from SITE-measurements.db --apply

    extract reads the copy (read-only) and writes a small file with one row per measured job: its number, submit time and measurements, nothing else. restore runs on the site: it fills in only jobs that have no measurement now, and only the same job: the submit times are known on both sides and agree (a job kept under NUMBER@SUBMIT when a later job took its number is found there). A value the copy doesn't have never replaces one stored. It counts the rest: measured since, another job under the same number, no submit time to tell (left alone), not on the site. Restored rows were measured the old way and the report counts them so (below). An older copy of the site's own database works as --from too, without extract. Not on the hub: the next sync brings them there. --apply first copies the database next to itself (--backup PATH elsewhere; SQLite's backup, safe while the collector writes) and stops if it can't.

    The report¶

    nomad impact report [--site SITE] [--source PART] [--advice-db PATH] [--json]

    --source is clusters, interactive, workstations, login, advice or all (the default: every part that has data).

    Clusters¶

    Month by month from baseline_from: jobs, people and how many were new, the share of jobs measured (of those that ran 10 s or more: the others' figures rest on them), CPU used of the core-hours measured jobs held, memory peak of what was asked for, time used of the time limits of completed jobs, the share that failed, timed out or ran out of memory, out-of-memory jobs, and the median wait from when a job could start. Events appear in their month. Then the periods side by side: before launch, after it, and the same days a year earlier where the data reaches back (students and courses follow the academic year). The figures are the ones the Console and nomad facts use. When more than a fifth of the measured jobs before launch were measured before 1.13 (CPU from AveCPU, which reads jobs running several tasks low), the report says their CPU can't be set against after launch; memory, time, failures and waits can. Counts and shares only: no names.

    Interactive servers¶

    From the interactive collector's daily totals: people, hours with at least one session open (in all and per person), the share of the measured time notebooks, kernels and RStudio sessions were computing, and their measured session-hours that weren't. The Jupyter server's own process isn't counted: it polls all the time, a few percent of a core, which until 1.13.2 made anyone with JupyterLab open look busy all the time (the report reads the sessions' own rows, so this holds for the history too). Days rebuilt from the raw snapshots (nomad interactive backfill) have hours but no CPU, so the computing share starts where the collector began keeping CPU; the report says from when.

    Lab workstations¶

    From the workstation collector's per-person snapshots: sessions (from signing in to the last program ending, counted in the month they ended), hours, core-hours used, long CPU-heavy runs (12 hours or more keeping half the machine's cores busy: what the Console advises moving to a cluster) and sessions whose programs held 80% or more of the machine's memory.

    A session is the person's slice on the machine (systemd's user-UID.slice) for as long as it lives. Until 1.13.2 sessions were told apart by the slice directory's change time, which the kernel moves whenever a login, terminal, ssh connection or cron job starts or ends there: one person's day came out as many sessions. Now a session runs while the slice's CPU time and memory peak keep growing, as they do until it is made again, and ends after two hours without a reading; this holds for the readings already collected too.

    A session can then last weeks (anything left running keeps the slice), so its average says little about a two-day job inside it. Long runs are found inside sessions (1.13.3): stretches in which every reading kept three quarters of the cores needed busy, with pauses of up to 30 minutes, and the whole stretch averaged half the machine's cores (at least 2) for 12 hours or more. Each run counts in the month it ended. The Console's advice to move long runs to a cluster uses the same runs.

    Memory is the programs' own (memory.stat anon + shmem, read from 1.13.2 on). memory.peak, all there was before, counts the file cache: a session that read large files looked as if it had filled the machine. Sessions without the new reading aren't counted as pressured or not (a dash), and the Console doesn't advise on their memory.

    Shared login hosts¶

    From the per_user collector: heavy-use episodes as nomad per-user merges them (and how many were actionable), the people flagged, everyone who used the host and the core-hours they used there.

    Advice shown and taken¶

    The Console notes, once a day, the advice it showed someone about their own work (Console fix 33 and later; the Console account's ~/.nomad-console/activity.db, or [impact] advice_db = "PATH", or --advice-db). For each piece of advice on a cluster partition, the person's typical request there in the 60 days before they first saw it and the 60 days after: "took it" when it moved at least half the way to the suggestion, "partly", "no change", or "too early" while there aren't three later jobs yet. For the advice to move long workstation runs to a cluster: whether the person's long runs fell to half or less and their jobs on that cluster grew. Plus how many people opened their own pages each month.

    There is no comparison group here (people with the same advice who never saw it), so part of a change may be requests drifting toward use anyway.

    Reading it¶

    What the report can say is what changed after launch, not why. The people and courses change from one term to the next, and the events changed things too. The closest thing to cause is whether a person's next jobs changed after they were shown advice (above). The other sources started at different times (workstations and login hosts only recently, at most sites), so their before periods can be short: nomad impact status says from when each has data, and the same months a year apart will say more than the first before and after.
